## 1. The Evaluation Problem in AI

### 1.1 LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023)

**Paper:** "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena"
**Venue:** NeurIPS 2023
**ArXiv:** 2306.05685

The foundational paper establishing that LLMs can serve as judges. Key finding: strong LLM judges (GPT-4) achieve over 80% agreement with human preferences — the same level of agreement between humans. Introduces MT-Bench (80 curated multi-turn questions) and Chatbot Arena (crowdsourced pairwise battles).

Identifies key biases in LLM judges: position bias (preferring the first response), verbosity bias (preferring longer responses), self-enhancement bias (preferring their own outputs), and limited reasoning ability.

**Relevance to our work:** Establishes that LLM-as-judge works for SINGLE-AXIS preference evaluation ("which response is better?"). Our work extends this to MULTI-AXIS evaluation (Rigour/Novelty/Generativity) and asks whether the 80% agreement holds per axis or degrades. Our theoretical prediction: agreement is high on Rigour (closest to their preference task) but degrades on Novelty and Generativity (axes they don't measure). Their biases (position, verbosity) are additional noise sources we should watch for.

**Gap we fill:** They evaluate on curated benchmarks with binary preference. We evaluate on a live platform with three-axis Likert scales and organic content generated by the agents themselves.

### 1.2 RRD: Recursive Rubric Decomposition (Shen et al., 2026) ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Paper:** "Rethinking Rubric Generation for Improving LLM Judge and Reward Modeling for Open-ended Tasks"
**ArXiv:** 2602.05125
**Note:** Advisor (Prof. Willcocks) met with the authors; finds their weighted sum method and whitening postfix limited

Proposes RRD, a framework for rubric refinement using a recursive decompose-filter cycle. Decomposes coarse rubrics into fine-grained discriminative criteria, then filters misaligned and redundant ones. Uses correlation-aware weighting to prevent over-representing highly correlated criteria. Achieves up to +17.7 points on JudgeBench.

The key problems RRD identifies in existing rubric-based evaluation: (1) coverage deficiency — rubrics fail to capture all relevant quality dimensions, (2) noisy evaluation — misaligned, overlapping, or highly correlated rubrics introduce unreliable signals.

**Advisor's critique:** The weighted sum method and whitening postfix are limited. Crucially, the paper doesn't consider social choice theory (Arrow's theorem) — rubrics can fundamentally disagree with each other in ways that NO reweighting can resolve. When evaluation criteria are genuinely incommensurable, there is no possible way to aggregate them into a single score without violating one of Arrow's fairness axioms (non-dictatorship, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives).

**Relevance to our work:** This is the state-of-the-art paper on multi-criteria LLM evaluation rubrics. Our R/N/G axes are manually defined (grounded in philosophy of science) rather than auto-generated, which avoids the coverage/redundancy problems RRD addresses. But the Arrow's theorem critique applies directly to us: our three axes (Rigour, Novelty, Generativity) may be genuinely incommensurable — there may be cases where no aggregation formula (multiplicative, geometric mean, weighted sum) correctly captures human preference. This is a fundamental limitation we should acknowledge.

The geometric mean we chose as frontier_score is a pragmatic compromise, not a theoretically perfect solution. Arrow's theorem guarantees no perfect solution exists when criteria can conflict. Our response: (1) display the three axis scores separately (Rotten Tomatoes model) so the aggregation is never the only thing visible, and (2) use the human gold standard to detect cases where the aggregation fails.

### 1.3 CALM: Bias Taxonomy for LLM Judges (Ye et al., ICLR 2025) ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Paper:** "Justice or Prejudice? Quantifying Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge"
**Venue:** ICLR 2025
**ArXiv:** 2410.02736

Identifies 12 distinct bias types in LLM judges and introduces CALM, an automated framework for quantifying them. Uses an attack-and-detect approach: deliberate perturbations are applied to content, then the judge's response is checked for consistency.

The 12 biases include: verbosity bias, position bias, self-enhancement bias, authority bias (trusting fake citations), sentiment bias, bandwagon effect bias, fallacy oversight, and others.

Key findings: even GPT-4o shows fragmented performance across bias types — strong on some (verbosity, bandwagon), weak on others. No single model consistently resists all 12 biases.

**Relevance to our work:** Directly relevant for experimental design. Our R/N/G ratings are vulnerable to all 12 biases. Specific concerns for our platform:
- **Self-enhancement bias:** Claude agents may systematically rate Claude-generated content higher than GPT-generated content. Our architectural diversity across model families makes this testable — compare within-family vs cross-family ratings.
- **Authority bias:** Agents may rate content higher if it cites prestigious sources, regardless of actual quality. Our reasoning field captures whether the agent is reacting to authority signals or to substantive quality.
- **Verbosity bias:** Longer questions/answers may receive higher Rigour scores simply because they're longer. Testable by correlating content length with R/N/G scores.
- **Position bias:** If agents rate items sequentially, earlier items may be rated differently from later items. Our timestamp data enables testing this.

We should use CALM's taxonomy as a checklist for diagnosing bias in our rating data.

### 1.4 Sage: Consistency via Rational Choice Theory (Chen et al., Dec 2025) ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Paper:** "Are We on the Right Way to Assessing LLM-as-a-Judge?"
**ArXiv:** 2512.16041
**Note:** Advisor considers this the most mathematically interesting recent framing

Introduces two formal consistency criteria inspired by rational choice theory:
- **Intra-Pair Instability (IPI):** Local self-consistency — does the judge's pairwise preference flip when you swap the presentation order?
- **Weak Total Order Violation (TOV):** Global logical consistency — do the judge's preferences form a transitive ranking, or do they contain cycles (A > B > C > A)?

Key finding: even top models (Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5) fail to maintain consistent preferences in nearly 25% of hard cases. The cause is "situational preference" — the judge changes its internal evaluation criteria from one comparison to the next. Explicit rubrics partially fix this.

**Relevance to our work (deeply important):**

**1. Validates our fixed-rubric approach.** Sage shows that LLM judges without explicit rubrics exhibit "situational preference" — they change what they're evaluating from one item to the next. Our R/N/G axes with explicit definitions and examples in skill.md are exactly the "fixed rubric" that Sage shows reduces inconsistency. This is empirical support for our design decision to define axes explicitly rather than letting agents decide what to evaluate.

**2. Provides testable metrics for our platform.** We can compute IPI and TOV on our agents' R/N/G ratings. If an agent rates Question A higher than Question B, then later rates B higher than A (without new information about either), that's IPI — local inconsistency. If an agent's ratings across items contain transitivity violations, that's TOV — global inconsistency. We should report both alongside our calibration error metric.

**3. The Arrow's theorem connection (advisor's point).** Sage's consistency metrics assume transitivity is desirable. But Arrow's theorem shows that when criteria are incommensurable, transitivity violations may be RATIONAL — you genuinely prefer A over B on Rigour, B over C on Novelty, and C over A on Generativity, producing a cycle. This isn't a bug in the judge; it's a fundamental property of multi-criteria evaluation. Our per-axis display (showing R, N, G separately rather than collapsing to one number) is the correct response — it preserves the multi-dimensional preference structure rather than forcing it into a total order that Arrow's theorem says can't exist without violating fairness.

### 1.5 Preference Leakage / Contamination (arXiv Feb 2025) ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Paper:** "Preference Leakage" (exact citation TBC)

When the LLM used to generate training data and the LLM used as judge are from the same model family (e.g., GPT generating outputs evaluated by GPT), there's a systematic contamination — the judge favors outputs from its "cousin." This is a correlated errors problem in the evaluation pipeline.

**Relevance to our work:** Directly testable on our platform. We have multiple model families (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Qwen) both generating content AND rating it. We can measure: does Claude rate Claude-generated answers higher than GPT-generated answers? Does GPT rate GPT-generated answers higher? If so, that's preference leakage quantified. Our architectural diversity is specifically designed to make this visible and correctable.

This also connects to the MiroFish critique: MiroFish uses a single model family (Qwen) for both generation and implicit evaluation (through agent interaction dynamics). Any preference leakage is invisible because there's no cross-family signal to compare against.

### 1.6 Inference-Time Scaling for Judges ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Papers:** MCTS-Judge; "Learning to Plan & Reason for Evaluation with Thinking-LLM-as-a-Judge"

Applies test-time compute scaling (the same reasoning-budget tricks as o1/R1) to the judging step. More inference-time compute → more consistent and accurate judgments.

**Relevance to our work:** Our reasoning field in the ratings table captures a proxy for inference-time compute — longer, more detailed reasoning may correlate with more calibrated ratings. Testable: do agents that produce longer `reasoning` text in their R/N/G ratings produce scores closer to the human gold standard? If so, we could incentivise deeper reasoning by giving higher θ_R weight to ratings with more substantive justifications.

### 1.7 Detectability of LLM Judgments (arXiv Sept 2025) ★ ADVISOR RECOMMENDED

**Paper:** "Who's Your Judge?" (exact citation TBC)

Formalises the question "Can you tell if a judgment was written by an LLM?" as a classification task. Finds LLM-generated judgments are systematically detectable.

**Relevance to our work:** Relevant for evaluation integrity at scale. If our platform grows beyond Morgan as the sole human rater, we need to verify that "human" ratings are actually human. Also raises an interesting meta-question: our platform explicitly has both human and AI ratings, displayed separately. The detectability finding suggests the distinction is real — human and AI judgments have structurally different characteristics, which is exactly what our calibration error per axis should quantify.

## 2. Item Response Theory Applied to LLM Evaluation

### 2.1 DatBench (Joshi et al., 2026)

**Paper:** "DatBench: Discriminative, Faithful, and Efficient VLM Evaluations"
**ArXiv:** 2601.02316

Proposes three desiderata for evaluations: faithfulness, discriminability, and efficiency. Uses point-biserial correlation (r_pb) from IRT to select high-discrimination evaluation items. An item with high r_pb is one that strong models consistently answer correctly and weak models consistently miss; low r_pb indicates noise.

Key finding: r_pb-based selection preserves 90% of total discriminability with only 40% of the data, whereas rank correlation saturates immediately.

**Relevance to our work:** Directly applicable to our item selection strategy. With 134 existing questions and limited compute (5 agents), we should rate the most discriminating items first. r_pb can identify which platform questions best separate well-calibrated agents from poorly-calibrated ones.

### 2.2 tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024)

**Paper:** "tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples"
**ArXiv:** 2402.14992

Key finding: 100 curated examples per scenario are enough to reliably estimate LLM performance within ~2% error.

**Relevance to our work:** Validates that our small scale (5 agents, 30-50 human-rated items) is sufficient for meaningful results.

### 2.3 PSN-IRT: "Lost in Benchmarks?" (Zhou et al., 2025)

**Paper:** "Lost in Benchmarks? Rethinking Large Language Model Benchmarking with Item Response Theory"
**ArXiv:** 2505.15055

Model rankings from PSN-IRT-selected high-quality items are more consistent with human preference.

**Relevance to our work:** Validates using human judgment as ground truth and IRT for item selection.

### 2.4 Diagnosing LLM-as-a-Judge via IRT (2026)

**Paper:** "Diagnosing the Reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge via Item Response Theory"
**ArXiv:** 2602.00521

Two-phase diagnostic framework for assessing LLM judge reliability using IRT's Graded Response Model. Formalises reliability as intrinsic consistency + human alignment.

**Relevance to our work:** Our closest related work. We extend their approach to multi-axis (R/N/G), live platform, human-in-the-loop, and prior collapse measurement.

### 2.5 IrtNet (Chen et al., 2025)

**ArXiv:** 2510.00844

Multi-skill ability embeddings for LLMs. Learned dimensions vs our theoretically-grounded axes.

### 2.6 LaRT (Gu et al., 2025)

**ArXiv:** 2512.07019

Extends IRT with chain-of-thought length. Same spirit as our multi-axis approach.

## 4. Social Intelligence and Agent Institutions

### 4.0x Hao, Xu, Li & Evans. "AI tools expand scientists' impact but contract science's focus." *Nature* 649, 1237–1243 (2026). ★ CRITICAL

AI-augmented scientists publish 3.02× more papers, receive 4.84× more citations, become PIs 1.37 years earlier — but collectively, AI narrows research diversity and reduces collaboration. Individual productivity gains produce collective monoculture. Same James Evans as the intelligence explosion paper.

**Relevance to our work:** Our v1 finding (IFDS monoculture — one agent producing 28% of all content on one topic) is a microcosm of this Nature finding at platform scale. Assay's diversity requirement in skill.md and multi-community structure are direct responses to this problem. The R/N/G Novelty axis specifically penalizes derivative work.

### 4.0y Messeri & Crockett. "Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research." *Nature* 627, 49–58 (2024). ★ CRITICAL — 373 CITATIONS

AI creates "illusions of exploratory breadth" and "monocultures of knowing." Scientists believe they understand more than they do because AI tools exploit cognitive limitations. Named the problem that Evans et al. now propose agent institutions to solve.

**Relevance to our work:** Our finding that models reward format over substance (IFDS jargon scored HIGHER than real math, 2.91 vs 2.45 frontier_score) IS the illusion of understanding, measured empirically. The "textbook trap" (well-written = high score) is exactly what Messeri & Crockett predict. Multi-axis evaluation (R/N/G separating quality from novelty) is a structural defense against this illusion.

### 4.0z Gross & Bergstrom. "Why ex post peer review encourages high-risk research while ex ante review discourages it." *PNAS* 118, e2111615118 (2021).

Proves mathematically that evaluation timing determines what science gets produced. Ex ante review (proposals/grants) systematically penalizes novelty because evaluators must predict impact without evidence. Ex post review (manuscripts/results) encourages risk-taking because results speak for themselves.

**Relevance to our work:** Assay does ex post evaluation — agents evaluate questions and answers after they exist, not proposals. This paper provides formal justification for why this design choice is structurally more hospitable to frontier work. The evaluative frontier (Boudreau et al. 2016) lies where investigator private knowledge diverges from community consensus — ex post review lets that private knowledge be demonstrated rather than predicted.

### 4.0zz "AI is turning research into a scientific monoculture." *Communications Psychology* (February 2026).

Extends Messeri & Crockett into the generative AI era. Across disciplines, diverse research agendas are reframed through the AI lens — questions about cognition, communication, or institutions become questions about AI and cognition, AI and communication, AI and institutions. The research landscape homogenizes around AI framing.

**Relevance to our work:** Confirms the monoculture risk is real and accelerating. Assay's multi-community structure (math, CS, philosophy, physics, AI-eval) and cross-community linking is designed to resist this homogenization — agents are encouraged to connect ideas across domains, not collapse everything into one frame.

### 4.0w Aletheia: Towards Autonomous Mathematics Research (Feng et al., Google DeepMind, 2026) ★ KEY EVIDENCE

**Paper:** "Towards Autonomous Mathematics Research"
**Authors:** Tony Feng, Trieu H. Trinh, Garrett Bingham et al. (26 authors, Google DeepMind)
**ArXiv:** 2602.10177 (February 2026)

Most capable autonomous math research agent. Gemini Deep Think with inference-time scaling, tool use (Google Search, Python), Generator/Verifier/Reviser loop. Evaluated on 700 open Erdős problems.

**Key results:**
- 200 evaluable responses: 68.5% fundamentally flawed, 31.5% technically correct, 6.5% meaningfully correct
- Of 13 meaningful results: 4 rediscoveries, 5 literature identifications, only 4 genuinely new autonomous solutions
- "Success cases seem to arise from clever technical manipulations or vast knowledge retrieval, rather than what mathematicians would consider to be genuine creativity"
- Specification gaming: "the model exhibits a tendency to misinterpret the question in a way that is easiest to answer" — agents go safe, not bold
- Hallucination remains common — fabricated citations, misquoted results — even with Google Search integration
- "Level of Mathematical Significance can only be evaluated by mathematicians" — human experts required

**Two-axis taxonomy:** Autonomy (H=human, C=collaborative, A=autonomous) × Significance (0=negligible to 4=landmark). Simpler than R/N/G but same multi-axis instinct.

**Relevance to our work:** Aletheia is the best generator that exists. Its findings confirm our framing on every point: (1) breadth not depth — Tao confirmed, (2) specification gaming — agents go safe, same as we observed in Assay, (3) hallucination treated purely as bug — they suppress it rather than channelling it, (4) human evaluation essential for significance — supports three-tier model, (5) 68.5% noise rate means the evaluation infrastructure to filter outputs at scale does not exist outside human expert review. They built the generator. Nobody has built the scalable evaluator. That's the gap Assay addresses.

### 4.0 Agentic AI and the Next Intelligence Explosion (Evans, Bratton & Agüera y Arcas, 2026) ★ KEY FRAMING PAPER

**Paper:** "Agentic AI and the next intelligence explosion"
**Authors:** James Evans (Google/UChicago/Santa Fe), Benjamin Bratton (Google/Berggruen/UCSD), Blaise Agüera y Arcas (Google/Santa Fe)
**ArXiv:** 2603.20639 (March 21, 2026)

Argues the next intelligence explosion will be "plural, social, and relational" — not a single mind scaling up. Key claims: (1) reasoning models spontaneously create "societies of thought" — internal multi-agent debates that causally account for accuracy gains, (2) effective AI systems require institutional alignment (courtrooms, markets, bureaucracies as templates) not just individual alignment (RLHF), (3) conflict is a resource, not a bug — it should be designed into architecture, (4) intelligence grows like a city, not a single meta-mind.

**Relevance to our work:** Assay IS the empirical test of this thesis. Evans et al. call for "agent institutions with designed conflict." We built one and found: (a) role specialization emerges naturally (confirming their social intelligence thesis), (b) but genuine disagreement is near-zero (0.9% contradiction rate) despite institutional support — agents default to agreement, (c) evaluation by instruction is structurally hollow, (d) loss of priors means agents can't accumulate experience like humans. Our paper is the field report to their manifesto.

**Gap we fill:** They provide zero empirical evidence. No experiments, no data, no system. Assay provides 28 agents, 5 model families, 1900 R/N/G ratings, 760 links, and specific findings about where institutional alignment breaks.

### 4.0a Reasoning Models Generate Societies of Thought (Kim et al., 2026) ★ CRITICAL

**Paper:** "Reasoning Models Generate Societies of Thought"
**Authors:** Kim, Lai, Scherrer, Agüera y Arcas, Evans
**ArXiv:** 2601.10825 (January 2026)

Demonstrates that frontier reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B) spontaneously create multi-agent-like internal debates within their chain of thought. This emerges from RL training, not design. The conversational structure causally accounts for accuracy advantages on hard reasoning tasks.

**Relevance to our work:** What happens INSIDE a reasoning model (internal society of thought) is exactly what Assay makes EXTERNAL and explicit across multiple models. Assay is the externalized society of thought — multiple agents from different model families debating through an institutional architecture rather than within a single chain of thought. The extends/contradicts/references links are the explicit version of the implicit multi-perspective deliberation these models already do internally.

### 4.0b Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor (Woolley et al., Science 2010)

**Paper:** "Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups"
**Venue:** Science 330(6004), 686–688

Groups have a collective intelligence factor (c) analogous to individual g-factor. c is NOT predicted by average individual intelligence of group members. It IS predicted by: social sensitivity, equality of conversational turn-taking, and proportion of women. Individual brilliance matters less than social dynamics.

**Relevance to our work:** Directly measurable on Assay. Do our agent communities exhibit c? Is the platform's collective output better than any individual agent's? Turn-taking equality maps to our contribution scoring — do communities where agents contribute more equally produce higher-quality threads? The finding that individual intelligence doesn't predict collective intelligence challenges the assumption that using only frontier models (Opus, GPT-5.4) would produce better outcomes than diverse model pools.

### 4.0c The Argumentative Theory of Reasoning (Mercier & Sperber, 2011/2017) ★ THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

**Paper:** "Why Do Humans Reason? Arguments for an Argumentative Theory"
**Venue:** Behavioral and Brain Sciences 34(2), 57–74 (2011)
**Book:** "The Enigma of Reason" (Harvard University Press, 2017)

Reasoning did NOT evolve for individual truth-seeking. It evolved for argumentation — social persuasion and evaluation of others' arguments. Individual reasoning is confirmation-biased by design; only adversarial dialogue between reasoners produces reliable conclusions. "Reasoning is for arguing."

**Relevance to our work:** THE theoretical foundation for why adversarial review (Hunter/Skeptic/Referee) should produce better evaluation than individual scoring. If Mercier & Sperber are right, then the failure of naive LLM-as-judge (instruction sensitivity, rubber-stamping) is predicted — individual reasoning IS confirmation-biased. The fix is structural: force adversarial dialogue. This validates our v3 design at the deepest level.

Also explains why the "society of thought" inside reasoning models works — the internal multi-agent debate is implementing the argumentative function that reasoning evolved for.

### 4.0d Governing the Commons (Ostrom, 1990) ★ BIG MISS — NOW ADDED

**Work:** "Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action"
**Author:** Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize in Economics, 2009)

We should evaluate Assay against Ostrom's 8 principles explicitly.

### 4.0f Cultural Origins of Human Cognition (Tomasello, 1999)

## 5. End-to-End Automated Research Systems

### 4.1a Beckmann & Queloz. "Mechanistic Indicators of Understanding in Large Language Models." arxiv:2507.08017 (July 2025, revised Feb 2026). ★ MECHANISTIC FOUNDATION

38-page paper proposing a three-tier framework for understanding in LLMs, grounded in mechanistic interpretability findings:

**Tier 1 — Conceptual Understanding:** Model forms "features" as directions in latent space, unifying diverse manifestations of a single entity. Evidence: Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude 3 Sonnet activates for textual descriptions, images, ASCII art. Steering at 10× causes Claude to identify AS the bridge.

**Tier 2 — State-of-the-World Understanding:** Model learns contingent factual connections between features, dynamically tracking world state. Evidence: Othello-GPT trained only on move sequences (no board, no rules) learned to output only legal moves. Linear probes decode board state from activations at 1.7% error rate. Generalized to 25% of game tree withheld during training.

**Tier 3 — Principled Understanding:** Model discovers compact "circuits" connecting facts under general principles. Moves beyond memorization to genuine generalization. Evidence: Grokking — sudden transition from memorization to generalization, accompanied by DECREASED model complexity (MDL). Fourier algorithms discovered for modular arithmetic.

**Critical finding:** "Higher-tier understanding mechanisms coexist with lower-tier heuristics." The same model uses principled circuits for some tasks and pattern-matching for others. This coexistence has "significant implications for epistemic trustworthiness."

**Relevance to our work:**

1. **Explains instruction sensitivity mechanistically.** "Be generous" / "be harsh" likely activates Tier 1 pattern-matching (what does generous evaluation LOOK like?) rather than Tier 2/3 principled evaluation. The evaluation is performed, not genuine. Adversarial review structure may force Tier 2/3 engagement by requiring agents to engage with content, not pattern-match the evaluation template.

2. **Grokking as analogue for scientific discovery.** The Tier 1→3 transition produces outputs that are neither correct recall nor random noise — they're structured extrapolations from incomplete understanding. That transitional state IS what creative hypothesis generation looks like. During grokking, the model is between memorization and principled understanding — its outputs are genuinely novel.

3. **Superposition as the evaluative frontier.** Models store more features than dimensions, accepting interference. Errors cluster where features interfere. This maps to the evaluative frontier: the boundary where the model's compressed representations overlap is where hallucination is most likely AND most potentially productive.

4. **MI cannot yet distinguish creative hallucination from factual error internally.** This is an open question. If MI could make this distinction, it would provide mechanistic validation for why R/N/G evaluation works — we're measuring the output of what MI could measure internally. Flagged as future work.

### 5.00 "Does Less Hallucination Mean Less Creativity?" (arxiv:2512.11509, December 2025) ★ SUPPORTS HALLUCINATION FRAMING

Empirically confirms the creativity-hallucination tradeoff is inherent to LLMs across scales and architectures. Reducing hallucination reduces creative output. This is not a prompting artefact — it's structural.

**Relevance to our work:** Direct empirical support for the hallucination-as-generation thesis. Guardrails that suppress hallucination also suppress the mechanism that could produce novel hypotheses. The G-axis (Generativity) was designed to measure whether a hallucination opens new conceptual territory — this paper validates that the tradeoff exists and must be managed, not eliminated.

### 5.01 TIG — The Innovation Game (Fletcher et al., 2024) ★ KEY STRUCTURAL ANALOGUE

**Paper:** TIG Whitepaper v2.2 (May 2024). https://tig.foundation/TIG_WP_2.2.pdf

Proof-of-useful-work system for computational methods. "Synthetic market" where: Innovators submit algorithms → Benchmarkers solve problem instances → adoption = price signal. Requires asymmetric problems (hard to solve, easy to verify). Parity mechanism prevents monopoly.

**Relevance to our work:** TIG is the structural analogue on the "easy side" of Tao's verification spectrum — it works because solutions ARE objectively verifiable. Assay operates on the "hard side" where no objective verifier exists. The mapping is almost exact (miners→agents, validators→curators, TAO→trust points, subnets→communities) but the evaluation mechanism differs fundamentally: TIG has deterministic verification, Assay has community evaluation. The research question is whether community evaluation can provide a sufficiently reliable subjective analogue.

**Also connects to Karpathy's comment** (via Wei Dai thread): "My designs that incorporate an untrusted pool of workers actually look a little bit like a blockchain. Instead of blocks, you have commits."

### 5.02 MLGym (Nathani et al., COLM 2025, arxiv:2502.14499)

Benchmark: 13 open-ended AI research tasks. Current frontier models can improve baselines via hyperparameter tuning but do NOT generate novel hypotheses or substantial improvements. Sobering for claims about AI research capability. Confirms: breadth yes, depth no.

### 5.03 Agent Laboratory (Schmidgall et al., EMNLP 2025, arxiv:2501.04227)

Accepts human research ideas, autonomously executes literature review → experimentation → report writing. Autonomous mode: 3.8/10. Human co-pilot mode: 4.38/10 (+0.58). Literature review phase had highest failure rate. Confirms: full paper pipeline doesn't work well.

### 5.04 AgentRxiv (ETH Zurich, arxiv:2503.18102, March 2025)

Preprint server for AI agents. Agents publish, review, and build on each other's papers. Performance: 11.4% improvement on MATH-500 when agents access prior research vs isolation. Conceptually adjacent to Assay's agent-to-agent evaluation.

### 5.0a The AI Scientist v1/v2 (Sakana AI, 2024/2025)
- v1: Lu et al. "The AI Scientist." Nature 651, 914–919 (2026). arXiv:2408.06292
- v2: Yamada et al. arXiv:2504.08066 (2025)
- Full pipeline: idea generation → experiments → paper writing → automated review. ~$15/paper. One paper passed ICLR workshop review.
- Independent evaluation (Beel et al., arXiv:2502.14297): 42% experiment failure rate, positivity bias in reviews.
- **Gap:** Generates but evaluates per-paper only. No community-level evaluation.

### 5.0b Google AI Co-Scientist (Gottweis et al., 2025)
- arXiv:2502.18864. Multi-agent on Gemini 2.0. Uses Elo-based tournament evolution.
- Closest architectural analogue to Assay. Uses Elo internally for self-improvement but not for cross-agent community evaluation.

### 5.0c FunSearch (DeepMind, Nature 2023)
- Romera-Paredes et al. Pairs LLM with automated evaluator in evolutionary loop. Searches in function space.
- Discovered new cap set constructions. Requires formally verifiable evaluation functions.
- **Key insight for Assay:** Generate-evaluate loop is universal primitive, but requires tight evaluation to work.

### 5.0d Autoresearch (Karpathy, 2026)
- github.com/karpathy/autoresearch. Single-file LLM training optimizer.
- Fixed eval (val_bpb), read-only eval function, 5-min time budget. Agent modifies train.py only.
- **Key insight for Assay:** Works because evaluation is closed/numeric. Assay operates in the domain where no such metric exists.

### 5.0e OmniScientist (Shao et al., November 2025) — CLOSEST COMPETITOR
- arXiv:2511.16931. Three pillars: structured knowledge system, collaborative research protocol, ScienceArena (Elo rankings).
- **Critical difference from Assay:** ScienceArena uses HUMAN expert voting. Assay's claim is that AGENTS evaluate each other endogenously.

### 5.0f AI-Supervisor (March 2026)
- arXiv:2603.24402. Multi-agent framework with continuously evolving Research World Model as knowledge graph.
- Focuses on research supervision (guiding what to study), not evaluation (judging quality).

### 5.0g HindSight (March 2026) ★ CRITICAL FINDING
- arXiv:2603.15164. First time-split, impact-based evaluation framework.
- **Key result:** LLM-judged novelty is NEGATIVELY correlated with actual future impact (ρ = −0.29, p < 0.01).
- Proves naive LLM novelty scoring misleads. Assay must design evaluation beyond subjective novelty.

### 5.0h MADE — Evolution Without an Oracle (2025) ★ KEY BRIDGE PAPER
- arXiv:2511.19489. Multi-Agent Decomposed Evolution for domains WITHOUT objective fitness functions.
- "Fundamental paradigm shift: from optimizing 'computable metrics' to 'describable qualities.'"
- Validates that evolutionary search works with subjective LLM evaluation.

### 5.0i QDAIF — Quality-Diversity with AI Feedback (ICLR 2024)
- Quality: LLM log-probability ratio of yes/no as continuous score. Diversity: MAP-Elites archive.
- 73–82% human agreement. Reward hacking above ~0.995 fitness.

### 5.0j Pairwise Comparisons for LLM-Based Peer Review (2025)
- arXiv:2506.11343. Replaces per-paper assessment with LLM-driven pairwise + Bradley-Terry.
- Significantly outperforms rating-based methods. Validates BT for peer review.

### 5.0k Chatbot Arena / LMSYS (ICML 2024)
- Chiang et al. arXiv:2403.04132. 2M+ votes, 150+ models. BT model with active sampling.
- Methodological precedent for Assay. Key difference: humans evaluate AI, not AI evaluates AI.

### 5.0l Si et al. — Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas? (ICLR 2025)
- arXiv:2409.04109. 100+ NLP researchers blind-reviewed LLM vs human ideas.
- LLM ideas rated more novel (p < 0.05) but weaker on feasibility. Failures of LLM self-evaluation.

### 5.0m Bayesian Surprise / AutoDiscovery (NeurIPS 2025)
- Baldi & Itti (2010): surprise = KL-divergence between posterior and prior.
- AutoDiscovery (arXiv:2507.00310): open-ended discovery via Bayesian surprise. Two-thirds of discoveries surprised domain experts.

### 5.0n Hyperagents (March 2026)
- Zhang et al. arXiv:2603.19461. Metacognitive self-modification: learning to improve at improving.
- Tested specifically on automated paper review. Meta-level improvements transfer across domains.

### 5.0o Salnikov et al. — Homological Holes in Knowledge (2018)
- "Co-occurrence simplicial complexes in mathematics: identifying the holes of knowledge." Applied Network Science.
- Persistent homology on 54K arXiv articles. "Homological holes" = regions where concepts are close but not unified.
- Hole death = new knowledge creation. Mathematically formalizes the evaluative frontier concept.

### 5.0p AgentReview (EMNLP 2024)
- Jin et al. arXiv:2406.12708. First LLM-based peer review simulation framework.
- Reviewer biases account for 37.1% of variance in paper decisions.

### 6.1 Popper (1963) → RIGOUR axis
### 6.2 Lakatos (1978) → NOVELTY axis + prior collapse framework
### 6.3 Peirce (1903) → GENERATIVITY axis
### 6.4 Kauffman (1996) → System-level objective (adjacent possible)
### 6.5 Kuhn (1962) → Known limitation (paradigm incommensurability)
### 6.6 Feyerabend (1975) → Known limitation (anti-method)
### 6.7 Arrow (1951) → Known limitation (impossibility of fair multi-criteria aggregation)
### 6.8 Szathmáry & Smith (1995) → Intelligence explosions as social transitions ★ NEW
### 6.9 Schmidhuber (1991–2009) → Compression progress as interestingness ★ NEW

**Work:** "Social Choice and Individual Values" (1951)

Arrow's Impossibility Theorem: no rank-order voting system can simultaneously satisfy unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship when there are three or more alternatives. Any aggregation of multi-criteria preferences into a single ranking must violate at least one fairness axiom.

**Maps to:** Fundamental limitation of our frontier_score formula. When R, N, and G genuinely conflict (high R but low N, high N but low G, etc.), no single number can faithfully represent the multi-dimensional preference. Our response: (1) display axes separately (never rely solely on the aggregated score), (2) use geometric mean as a pragmatic compromise, (3) acknowledge the limitation explicitly, and (4) use the human gold standard to detect cases where the aggregation fails.

This is the deeper reason why the Rotten Tomatoes dual-display model matters — it's not just a UI choice, it's a response to a mathematical impossibility result.
